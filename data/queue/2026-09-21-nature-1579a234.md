# 2026-09-21-nature-1579a234

## Cover

**Kicker:** Biological Psychiatry Global Open Science
**Headline:** Breathing polluted air **may reshape how teenage brains respond to fear**, a pattern seen in anxiety disorders.

## Slide 1 - What it means for you

**Title:** Air pollution might be getting inside the anxiety loop, not just the lungs.

**Basis:** inferred    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

Adolescents breathing higher pollution showed fear responses typical of anxiety disorders. Their brains stayed on high alert even after a threat was gone.

If pollution alters fear learning during adolescence, it could help explain rising anxiety rates in polluted areas.

## Slide 2 - The result

**Title:** Teens exposed to higher PM2.5 pollution showed stronger fear responses across three separate tests.

Those above EPA's annual limit showed greater skin conductance and reported more fear during conditioning. During recall, they showed a bigger return of fear to already-safe cues.

Brain scans showed more amygdala and insula activation, regions central to fear processing.

## Slide 3 - How it works

**Title:** Researchers tracked 102 adolescents through fear conditioning, extinction, and recall over one month.

Participants learned to fear a cue, then learned it was safe, then were tested days later. Past-month pollution exposure was estimated using spatial models.

Those above versus below EPA limits differed at every stage.

## Caveats

- This study found a pattern, not a cause. Something else about high-pollution neighborhoods could explain the link.
- The threshold used was EPA's annual standard, measured over one month of exposure in this study.
- The implications for anxiety risk are inferred. The paper measured fear responses, not diagnosed anxiety disorders.
