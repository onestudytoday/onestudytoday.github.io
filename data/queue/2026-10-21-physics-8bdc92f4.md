**Headline:** An extremely warped orbit could mean **binary stars collide more unpredictably** than models allow

## Slide 1 - Why you should care

**Title:** If this orbit survived, current models may badly underestimate how many colliding neutron stars we are missing.

**Basis:** inferred    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

Astronomers use star evolution models to predict how many neutron star pairs exist and what their orbits look like. This system was predicted to occur less than once in two hundred discoveries.

Finding it suggests the models undercount rare orbits. That matters for gravitational wave searches.

## Slide 2 - What the data says

**Title:** The team reports a neutron star pair in an orbit with eccentricity 0.992, circling once every 79 days.

A perfect circle has eccentricity zero. This is among the most elongated orbits ever recorded for a neutron star binary.

The pulsar swings past at 1.14 million meters per second, then coasts out slowly.

## Slide 3 - Why the difference

**Title:** The extreme stretch can only come from a violent kick when the second star exploded as a supernova.

When a massive star collapses, the newborn neutron star can be flung at high speed. A large, mistimed kick warps the orbit drastically.

The probability of detecting a system like this is less than 0.005, challenging current evolution theory.

## Caveats

- This is a preprint. It has not been peer reviewed, so no independent expert has checked the work yet.
- The detection probability estimate depends on existing models, which this discovery itself calls into question.
- The system is inferred from pulsar timing data. Direct imaging of the neutron stars is not possible at this distance.
