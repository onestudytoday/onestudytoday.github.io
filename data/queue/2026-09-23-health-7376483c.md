## Cover

**Kicker:** DEN open · Prospective cohort
**Headline:** **An electrical test could flag harder-to-treat stomach cancers** during the procedure that removes them

## Slide 1 - What it means for you

**Title:** Electrical readings from tissue could help doctors spot invasion depth they cannot see on a camera.

**Basis:** inferred    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

Gastric cancer that has pushed into deeper layers is riskier and harder to treat. Cameras show surface patterns, but not what is happening underneath.

This test might flag those hidden changes in real time, while the tumor is being removed.

## Slide 2 - The result

**Title:** Tissue resistance in removed stomach tumors was linked to both deeper invasion and sores.

Tumors that had invaded the lower layer showed resistance of 6.9 Ω, compared to 9.7 Ω in those limited to the surface. Tumors with sores also had lower readings.

The pattern held after adjusting for tumor type and protein markers.

## Slide 3 - How it works

**Title:** The test tracked claudin-18, a junction protein that changes as cancer advances.

Resistance was measured on specimens right after removal. Researchers used both high- and low-frequency current. Only low-frequency readings matched invasion depth and sore status.

Claudin-18 was the sole independent factor in analysis.

## Caveats

- This study found a pattern, not a cause. Something else could explain the link between tissue resistance and tumor behavior.
- Tests were done on removed specimens after the procedure, not during live camera work, so real-time use is not yet validated.
- Single-center study; the findings need testing in other hospitals and with different patient groups before broader use.
