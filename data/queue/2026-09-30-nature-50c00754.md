## Cover

**Kicker:** Behavioural Brain Research + meta-analysis
**Headline:** **Stopping an unwanted memory and unlearning fear** use some of the same brain systems to regulate the past.

## Slide 1 - Why you should care

**Title:** Two ways of controlling the past—one deliberate, one learned—turn out to share neural systems.

**Basis:** stated    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

When you try to block a memory and when you unlearn a fear response, your brain recruits the same systems. Both regulate how the past affects you now.

The same circuits that help you shut down an intrusive thought may also help you stop reacting to what used to scare you.

## Slide 2 - What the data says

**Title:** A combined analysis of 63 brain-imaging studies found shared activity in regions tied to control and attention.

Memory blocking and fear unlearning both engaged the inferior frontal gyrus, anterior insula, and inferior parietal lobule. These regions sit within networks tied to executive control and attention.

Distinct patterns in memory and emotion areas show key differences remain between the two processes.

## Slide 3 - The assumption

**Title:** Memory blocking and fear unlearning are studied separately, as though they are entirely distinct processes.

They draw on different research traditions. One asks people to deliberately block recall. The other trains threat cues to become safe through exposure.

But both reduce how past experience affects current behavior. The analysis tested whether they recruit shared neural systems.

## Caveats

- The combined analysis pooled studies that used different tasks and designs, which may introduce noise despite efforts to focus on comparable contrasts.
- Shared brain activity does not mean the regions perform identical functions in both processes, only that both recruit them.
- The findings describe group-level patterns across studies and may not reflect how regulation works in any single person.
