## Cover

**Kicker:** arXiv · 12.3 km resolution
**Headline:** A solar telescope has captured **spinning plasma vortices smaller than any model predicted** could be seen.

## Slide 1 - Why you should care

**Title:** The telescope gives scientists their first direct look at the tiny spinning vortices that theory says drive solar energy upward.

**Basis:** stated    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

These spinning structures may transport energy and magnetic twist from the sun's surface into its outer atmosphere. Models predicted they exist at 50 to 100 km across, but no tool could see them.

DKIST's sharpness is 12.3 km. That opens a window into how the sun's smallest spinning flows behave.

## Slide 2 - What the data says

**Title:** The team reports 201 vortex detections in 3.2 minutes, with a median diameter of 38.6 km.

Virtually all detections were smaller than 50 km, placing them in the regime that models predict but no one had observed. Median swirling period was 37.3 seconds.

There was no preference for one spin direction over the other. That fits theory: Coriolis force is too weak at these scales.

## Slide 3 - The assumption

**Title:** Previous solar telescopes lacked the sharpness to confirm that vortices this small exist.

DKIST is a 4-meter telescope observing at 416 nanometers. It analyzed a bright patch on the sun's surface containing concentrated magnetic fields.

The observations cover a field of view of 4.92 by 4.13 megameters across 143 frames.

## Caveats

- This is a preprint. It has not been peer reviewed, so no independent expert has checked the work yet.
- This study found a pattern, not a cause. Something else could explain the link between vortex size and the magnetic environment.
