## Cover

**Kicker:** arXiv preprint, atomic clocks
**Headline:** Two atomic clocks 2,220 km apart **now agree to within a second every 6 billion years**.

## Slide 1 - What it could mean

**Title:** This early paper reports a milestone toward changing how the SI second is defined, using light instead of microwaves.

**Basis:** stated    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

The test reached a fractional uncertainty below 5×10⁻¹⁸. The team says this is an important step toward an optical change to the SI second.

If the approach holds, timekeeping could shift from today's clocks to optical ones.

## Slide 2 - Results

**Title:** The two clocks showed a fractional frequency difference of 1.0 plus or minus 4.8 times 10⁻¹⁸.

Both clocks use a single ytterbium ion. They were compared remotely via a 2,220 kilometre optical fibre link using over one million seconds of data.

The uncertainty was dominated by relativistic redshift correction between the two sites.

## Slide 3 - What they did

**Title:** Both clocks measure the same energy jump in a ytterbium ion, built independently by teams in the UK and Germany.

The energy jump is a specific shift between levels in the ion. Measuring its frequency gives an extremely stable tick.

The test also let the team search for violations of Lorentz invariance.

## Caveats

- This is a preprint. It has not been peer reviewed, so no independent expert has checked the work yet.
- The measurement uncertainty was dominated by relativistic redshift correction, which includes time-varying gravity potential differences between the two sites.
- The clocks use a single ion species and one energy jump, so this does not yet confirm agreement across different optical clock designs.
