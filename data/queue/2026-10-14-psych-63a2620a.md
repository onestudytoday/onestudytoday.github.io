# 2026-10-14-psych-63a2620a

## Cover

**Kicker:** Journal of Experimental Child Psychology
**Headline:** **When a robot sounds like it knows things, kids question it less** - even if the claim is wrong.

## Slide 1 - Why you should care

**Title:** Authority cues can shape how much evidence children seek before accepting what a robot tells them.

**Basis:** stated    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

As robots become tutors and learning partners, we need to know whether children still check what they hear. If a robot that sounds expert stops kids from asking questions, it could weaken the checking habits they need.

This study measured whether authority changes how children test a robot's claims.

## Slide 2 - What the data says

**Title:** Chinese children aged four and six checked a robot's claims less when it was presented as knowing things.

Researchers told 328 children that all aliens in a task had three eyes, using robots with different cues. When the robot seemed to know things, kids checked the claim less often and for less time.

Role cues - teacher versus peer - had no effect.

## Slide 3 - The assumption

**Title:** You might expect children to question robots the same way they question people, or even more so.

Robots are tools, not social equals. It would make sense if kids treated them like books - useful but worth checking.

Instead, the same cues that make us defer to human experts worked on a machine.

## Caveats

- The study was done in China with Chinese children. Cultural attitudes toward robots and authority may differ elsewhere.
- The task used a simple claim about cartoon aliens, not real schoolwork or advice that could actually mislead a child.
