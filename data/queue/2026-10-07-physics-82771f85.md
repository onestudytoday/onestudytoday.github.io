## Cover

**Kicker:** arXiv (preprint, not peer reviewed)
**Headline:** A new detector array could help explain **how lightning produces rare bursts of gamma rays** visible from space.

## Slide 1 - What it means for you

**Title:** If this imaging system works, it may let scientists watch the exact moment a lightning bolt creates high-energy radiation.

**Basis:** inferred    <!-- stated = the paper says it; inferred = our read, must stay conditional -->

Terrestrial gamma-ray flashes are rare bursts of radiation linked to the start of lightning. Researchers still don't know how they form.

The team is building BOLT, a radio array that maps lightning in 3D alongside detectors that have already caught gamma flashes.

## Slide 2 - The result

**Title:** The BOLT array uses radio detectors sensitive to frequencies between 30 MHz and 80 MHz to image lightning propagation.

The team reports that the array is now in commissioning. Station firmware has been modified to capture and stream multi-second waveforms.

The system sits at Pierre Auger Observatory, which has already recorded signals tied to gamma-ray flashes.

## Slide 3 - How it works

**Title:** BOLT builds on VHF radio detectors originally developed for the Auger Engineering Radio Array.

VHF stands for very high frequency radio waves. These detectors map where and when lightning emits radio signals as it branches through a storm.

By matching that 3D picture to gamma-ray signals, the team hopes to pinpoint which part of a discharge goes with the flash.

## Caveats

- This is a preprint. It has not been peer reviewed, so no independent expert has verified the work yet.
- This paper describes a system still in commissioning. The array has not yet produced the coincidence measurements it was built to capture.
- This study found a pattern linking gamma-ray flashes to lightning. That does not show one causes the other.
- The implications on slide one are our extrapolation of what this tool may enable, not a claim the authors make in the abstract.
